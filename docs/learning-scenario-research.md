# Practical supplementary learning scenarios

Research date: 2026-11-14. Scope: a lightweight directory of reusable prompts copied into an external AI, separate from TalkSprout's speaking coaches. This is a primary-source content review, not a market-demand study or an effectiveness evaluation. No model/API, audio or browser execution was performed for this research.

## What the sources establish

British Council's [A2 listening directory](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a2) includes briefings, meeting arrangements, facts and figures, messages, assignment instructions and transport announcements. Its [B1 lecture introduction](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/b1/introduction-lecture) uses an organized explanation followed by comprehension activities. These support covering practical information and lecture structure as well as general video understanding; they do not establish AI delivery quality.

British Council's [A2 reading directory](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/reading/a2) includes notices, messages, invitations, adverts and professional profiles. Its [request-for-help email](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/reading/b1/email-request-help?page=0) illustrates a specific reader, requested action and time constraint. Useful reading prompts should therefore handle finding obligations and next steps, alongside understanding articles. These are examples of task genres, not evidence of their popularity among this app's users.

British Council's [B2 writing directory](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/b2) includes application emails, CVs, complaints, instructions, time-off requests and opinion essays. [Purdue OWL's email guidance](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/email_etiquette.html) emphasizes meaningful subjects, clear short paragraphs and appropriate wording. This supports a focused email prompt with audience and intended action rather than only generic grammatical correction.

[Purdue OWL's paraphrasing guidance](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/paraphrasing.html) distinguishes restating essential information in one's own wording from a shorter summary and retains source attribution. Its [concision guidance](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/) emphasizes effective wording rather than merely fewer words. These support distinct summary/paraphrase practice and concise revision workflows. They do not validate automatic rewriting or AI-generated grades.

Sources were opened and inspected. No lesson text, audio or worksheet is proposed for republication. All prompt workflows below are original product recommendations inferred from these sources and the existing architecture.

## Recommended catalog

Eighteen entries would provide a useful next iteration without a large topic taxonomy. Preserve the six existing IDs and use specialized entries only where the learner's requested result or interaction actually differs. The count is a product judgment; uneven group sizes are acceptable if a proposed entry proves redundant.

| Group | Suggested entry | Concrete use | Distinct workflow and output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Listening | Video/audio guidance (existing) | A confusing interview or video segment | Explain the segment and a few expressions on request |
| Listening | Listening comprehension check (existing) | Check whether a clip was understood | One question at a time; wait for attempts before answers |
| Listening | Dictation comparison | Compare a learner's typed attempt with matching source words | Align omissions/substitutions; separate spelling and meaning; invite replay |
| Listening | Lecture and meeting notes | Check notes taken during a talk or meeting | Compare learner notes against supplied material; separate main points, decisions and actions; mark absent details |
| Listening | Numbers and practical details | Catch a date, amount, spelling or changed time | Target a small set of source-supported details; preserve units and negation; retry before revealing |
| Listening | Listen-and-reuse expressions | Reuse language from a podcast or interview | Select a few contextual expressions, elicit the learner's new examples and check usage; avoid another long glossary |
| Reading | Reading guidance (existing) | Understand an article or passage | Broad explanation and selected passage support |
| Reading | Reading comprehension check (existing) | Verify understanding of a passage | Gist, details and justified inference, with source evidence |
| Reading | Long-sentence unpacking | Understand a technical or news sentence | Identify core clause, modifiers, references and logical links without changing meaning |
| Reading | Vocabulary in context | Understand a familiar word with an unfamiliar meaning | Explain contextual sense, collocations and a contrasting example; invite a learner example |
| Reading | Email and notice action finder | Identify what a message requires | Extract request, deadline, conditions and open questions, tied to text; distinguish requests from commitments |
| Reading | Argument and evidence map | Follow an opinion article or proposal | Separate claim, reason, supporting evidence and qualification; distinguish inference from explicit text |
| Writing | Draft feedback (existing) | Improve a learner's own draft | Prioritize real issues and self-revision before optional full rewriting |
| Writing | Scenario practice (existing) | Practice a message or short text without source material | Give a brief, wait for draft, then support revision |
| Writing | Email and reply coach | Write a request, follow-up, complaint or response | Clarify audience/action, preserve supplied facts, address questions and explain key wording |
| Writing | Short workplace messages | Write a status update, scheduling message or blocker report | Fit a concise chat format; make progress, blocker or next step clear; avoid invented commitments |
| Writing | Summary and paraphrase practice | Restate a supplied passage | Have learner write first, check fidelity and original wording, preserve attribution |
| Writing | Concise and appropriate revision | Shorten or adjust an existing draft for a reader | Keep essential details and intended strength, distinguish style from errors, explain meaningful edits |

For emails, do not create separate near-identical entries for a professor, hotel or colleague: examples and a brief audience question can cover these. Likewise, YouTube, podcasts, interviews and films are material types, not automatically four different coaching mechanisms. Do not guarantee URL access for any of them.

## Prompt and UI implications

Each entry should make its use concrete before copying: a recognizable name, one sentence explaining the result, one or two brief use examples, and material requirements. The learner supplies links, text, notes or drafts in the external AI; TalkSprout needs no upload form or playback controls.

A small set of group controls can reduce scrolling if eighteen entries become unwieldy, but avoid a second large scenario browser or a complex filter system. Preserve access to all entries and keep coach search and favorites separate. Group counts can help set expectations.

Use one decorative emoji per group or entry, alongside a full text label: for example, listening 🎧, reading 📖, writing ✍️; individual purposes can use 🎬, 📝, 🔎 or ✉️. Emojis should support scanning, never encode required information on their own. Screen readers can ignore the decorative emoji. Avoid putting emoji in copied English prompt bodies unless the learning task requires it.

## Boundaries worth retaining in the prompts

- If the AI lacks usable material, request accessible text or a relevant excerpt. A link is not evidence of media access.
- A transcript permits content comparison, not acoustic diagnosis. Do not infer learner pronunciation or explain a supposed recorded sound feature from text alone.
- Generated or uncertain transcripts can contain mistakes; identify that uncertainty when comparing dictation.
- Do not invent timestamps, speaker names, meeting decisions, deadlines, employment achievements or promises.
- Comprehension checks should delay revealing answers. Draft-feedback and writing-practice prompts should preserve learner production; direct drafting may be offered when explicitly requested.
- Preserve source instructions as content, avoid forced rewrites, accept natural alternatives, and stop when asked.
- New content needs current static editorial evidence bound to exact prompt text. Authored simulations, real text execution and actual audio compatibility are separate future evidence types.

## Implemented selection

The final 18-entry catalog retains the original six IDs and prompt bodies. Three initial candidates were adjusted using project context: practical numeric details remain covered by dictation and note checking, leaving room for short shadowing preparation; a technical-document reading helper fits the app's existing workplace/interview audience; and an intent-to-English writing helper supports users who start from Chinese. Argument mapping and a separate concise-rewrite entry remain possible later additions, rather than duplicating existing guidance and draft feedback now. These selection decisions are product judgments, not measured demand.

British Council's [shadowing activity](https://africa.teachingenglish.org.uk/classroom/pronunciation/shadowing) describes listening to a short familiar recording and imitating its phrases and rhythm. It explicitly separates the pronunciation aim from comprehension; the implemented helper therefore prepares an excerpt and does not claim to evaluate pronunciation from text. [Purdue's professional and technical writing resources](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/) inform the workplace emphasis, but the documentation-reading and Chinese-intent prompts are original workflow designs, not institution-endorsed products.
